Overview

This project is a custom-built Digital Forensics Capture the Flag (CTF) event that I created and ran for the undergraduate Digital Forensics class at USCB.

Problem / Goal

The goal was to design a realistic, educational CTF that would genuinely challenge students while still being built so that everyone in the class could find some level of success.

Approach

Working with the professor of the Digital Forensics class, Dr. Comitz, we settled on a 10-flag event. The majority of the flags — 7 of them — were hidden in standalone files. The last 3 were contained in a single file that required a multi-step process to uncover.

Results / Outcome

The event was a success. Every student who put in effort found some level of success. There were some issues with time, but that was on the students — they had multiple days of 24/7 access to the files. The event itself ran with minimal problems, a huge improvement over last year's CTF (Rogue Telnet). The biggest problem was that one of the flags ran off a web server, and students were interacting with it from Raspberry Pis. For whatever reason, the Pis (or the USCB network) have trouble resolving nmap scans — the same issue I hit during last year's Rogue Telnet event. Related to that, the Pis also struggled to hold an internet connection, which became a problem when students were submitting their screenshots. The easy fix was to have them move their files off the Pis with a flash drive and onto their normal computers.

What I Learned & Reflection

I learned that building a CTF that caters to many skill levels is genuinely difficult. It's easy to create something everyone can finish, but then a handful of students fly through it without any struggle. The flip side — making it very hard — means only a few can make progress and most of the class ends up frustrated. Finding the balance between those two extremes is the hard part, and this event landed a little too far on the hard side. About half the class found 9–10 flags and the other half landed around 6–7. I'm still not sure how I could have made it much easier: the students who did well finished in under an hour, while the ones who struggled spent nearly two full class periods (90 minutes) and still didn't find everything. I did build something for the students who blew through it quickly, but I cover that in a separate project.
