Overview

Mystery.jpeg CTF is a continuation of the Digital Forensics CTF. I built it as a challenge for any student who managed to complete the Digital Forensics CTF — a much harder event that required learning new tools and methods.

Problem / Goal

My goal was to create something genuinely difficult, even something that wasn't necessarily achievable. I think it's important to build resilience when tackling hard problems, and this was a great way to do that. It also gave me a way to challenge the high performers: because I'd designed the Digital Forensics CTF to be achievable for the whole class, I hadn't been able to stretch the students at the top. Mystery.jpeg fixed that.

Approach

I built the entire CTF around a single image. That image contained a zip file embedded with Least Significant Bit (LSB) steganography, and inside the zip was an assortment of files that each held a flag. The CTF didn't have much of a flow to it — it was more a bundle of standalone challenges — and some of the flags were nearly impossible to identify without a hint. The fun twist was that I didn't hide flags so much as pieces of evidence, which the student then used to solve a mystery: who hacked it? I took direct inspiration from Murdle and whodunits, and it turned the CTF into something like a puzzle box. It was a fun addition that didn't really change the mechanics — students just had one extra logic puzzle to solve at the end.

Results / Outcome

Two students had real success with this CTF. One made it about halfway through, and another completed it — though I had to guide them through certain parts. Overall I consider it an absolute success. I didn't expect many people to even attempt it, and I wasn't sure it was at a level undergrads could get through at all. On guiding that one student: I had two flags that weren't quite created equal to the rest. The first was hidden directly in the mystery.jpeg image — the text is uncovered by running a forward Fourier transform on the image, with evidence hidden in the resulting frequency domain. I'm honestly not sure how a student would have connected the dots to run a Fourier transform; the only hint was some strange-looking corners of the image. The second tricky flag was a polyglot file: it was a valid JPEG, a valid zip, and a carrier for raw DEFLATE-compressed data all at once. Without going into too much detail, you had to realize the file held far more than met the eye, recognize the DEFLATE-compressed data, and inflate it. The inflated data then gave you instructions to build a key and IV to decrypt a .enc file containing the final piece of evidence. I didn't build that flag expecting anyone to find it — it was mostly me playing with ideas I'd just learned. It was the only flag the student who finished didn't complete, but they made an educated guess from the rest of the evidence they'd gathered and still solved the mystery.

What I Learned

I learned that I really enjoy creating puzzles and challenges. I find it genuinely engaging, and building this CTF was nothing but fun for me. I got to learn a lot about polyglot files, which I'd had very little experience with. I also learned that not everyone shies away from a very difficult challenge — some people run straight at it. I want to be around the people who run at it.
